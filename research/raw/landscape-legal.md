## Right of publicity and related law

- **[CONFIRMED]** C1: The right of publicity protects a person's identity (such as name, likeness and voice) from unauthorized commercial use. The rules differ a lot between states, and there is no general federal right yet. - https://rightofpublicityroadmap.com/ , https://www.dmlp.org/legal-guide/using-name-or-likeness-another - General background, consistent across both sources.
- **[CONFIRMED]** C2: A claim generally needs three things: use of an identifying trait, for an exploitative (usually commercial) purpose, without consent. News, commentary and transformative creative uses get broad First Amendment protection. Privacy claims end at death, but the publicity right continues after death in many states. - https://www.dmlp.org/legal-guide/using-name-or-likeness-another - Opened.
- **[CONFIRMED]** C3: Ohio R.C. 2741.02 (current version effective April 7, 2009) bars using any part of a person's persona for a commercial purpose without written consent. Protection lasts for the person's lifetime and 60 years after death (10 years for deceased military members). - https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-2741.02 - Statute opened.
- **[CONFIRMED]** C4: Ohio defines "persona" as name, voice, signature, photograph, image, likeness or distinctive appearance, when it has commercial value. "Name" includes any clearly identifying reference to a living or dead person. "Commercial purpose" includes use with products or services, in advertising, and in fundraising. - https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-2741.01 - Statute opened.
- **[CONFIRMED]** C5: The Ohio statute covers people domiciled or resident in Ohio on or after November 22, 1999, and people who died on or after January 1, 1998 while domiciled in Ohio. - https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-2741.03 - Statute opened.
- **[CORRECTED]** C6: Ohio R.C. 2741.09 exempts literary, dramatic, fictional, historical, audiovisual and musical works; material with political or newsworthy value; original fine art; ads for those exempt works; truthful crediting of someone as author or performer; reporting on events or topics of public interest; and a few other narrow cases. A separate catch-all exempts uses protected by the First Amendment, but only if the use does not convey or reasonably suggest that the person endorses it. - https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-2741.09 - The statute text shows the "no suggested endorsement" condition applies to the First Amendment catch-all in (A)(6), not to every exemption.
- **[CONFIRMED]** C7: Ohio remedies: actual damages including the defendant's profits, or statutory damages of $2,500 to $10,000. Punitive damages, attorney fees, injunctions, impoundment and treble damages against knowing parties are also available. There is a 4-year deadline to sue. - https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-2741.07 - Statute opened.
- **[CONFIRMED]** C8: Virginia Code § 8.01-40 bars using a person's name, portrait, picture, voice or likeness for advertising or trade without written consent. After death, the surviving spouse (or next of kin if none) can sue, but not more than 20 years after the death. Punitive damages are available for knowing use. - https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title8.01/chapter3/section8.01-40/ , https://rightofpublicityroadmap.com/state_page/virginia/ - Statute opened.
- **[CONFIRMED]** C9: Virginia protects publicity by statute only. WLJA-TV v. Levin, 564 S.E.2d 383 (Va. 2002), held there is no common-law right of publicity. - https://rightofpublicityroadmap.com/state_page/virginia/ - Secondary source. The case itself was not opened.
- **[CONFIRMED]** C10: California Civil Code § 3344 bars knowingly using another person's name, voice, signature, photograph or likeness on products or in advertising without consent. Damages are the greater of $750 or actual damages, plus profits, with punitive damages and fees available. News, public affairs, sports and political campaigns are exempt. - https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=3344 - Statute opened.
- **[CORRECTED]** C11: California Civil Code § 3344.1 protects deceased personalities for 70 years. Plays, books, audiovisual works and similar content are exempt unless the use directly promotes a product. AB 1836 (Stats. 2024, ch. 258, effective January 1, 2025) added digital-replica rules with minimum damages of $10,000. Right of Publicity Roadmap reports that the Ninth Circuit limited § 3344.1 to people who died domiciled in California. - https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=3344.1 , https://rightofpublicityroadmap.com/state_page/california/ - AB 1836 is a 2024 statute that took effect in 2025, not a 2025 update. The Roadmap page names no case for the domicile holding, so that point is secondary only.
- **[CONFIRMED]** C12: New York Civil Rights Law § 50-f protects people who died domiciled in New York, for 40 years after death. S.8882, signed and effective December 11, 2025, extended digital-replica protection to deceased personalities, not only performers. It removed the old disclaimer defense and the "likely to deceive" requirement, and set minimum damages of $2,000. - https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2026/01/two-newly-enacted-new-york-laws-will-regulate , https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/CVR/50-F - Current statute text shows no disclaimer defense. The details of the old defense come from a law-firm summary.
- **[CONFIRMED]** C13: New York S.8420, signed December 11, 2025 and effective about June 9, 2026 (180 days later), requires a conspicuous disclosure when an advertiser knowingly uses a generative-AI "synthetic performer" in an ad. Penalties are $1,000 for a first violation and $5,000 after that. Audio-only ads, expressive works, translation and platforms carrying third-party content are excluded. - https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2026/01/two-newly-enacted-new-york-laws-will-regulate - Law-firm summary only.
- **[CONFIRMED]** C14: Indiana protects publicity rights for 100 years after death, whatever the person's domicile, when the use happens in Indiana. - **[CONFIRMED]** C15: Tennessee's ELVIS Act (effective July 1, 2024) protects voice, including a simulated voice. It creates liability for distributing technology whose primary purpose is to reproduce a specific person's photograph, voice or likeness without authorization. Violations can be sued civilly or prosecuted as a Class A misdemeanor. News, commentary, criticism, satire and parody are exempt. - https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2024/04/first-of-its-kind-ai-law-addresses-deep-fakes-and-voice-clones - Law-firm summary.
- **[CORRECTED]** C16: The NO FAKES Act of 2026 (S.4591, sponsor Sen. Coons; House companion H.R.8915) was introduced May 20, 2026. - **[CONFIRMED]** C18: 15 U.S.C. § 1125(a)(1)(A) makes it actionable to use a name, word or symbol in commerce in a way likely to confuse people about affiliation, sponsorship or approval by another person. - **[CONFIRMED]** C19: Nominative fair use comes from New Kids on the Block v. News America Publishing, 971 F.2d 302 (9th Cir. 1992). - **[CONFIRMED]** C20: 15 U.S.C. § 1115(b)(4) allows descriptive fair use, meaning good-faith, non-brand use of a term to describe your own goods. § 1125(c)(3)(A) excludes nominative and descriptive fair use from dilution claims. - https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/1115 , https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/1125 - Standard statutory text, not re-fetched this pass.
- **[CONFIRMED]** C21: 17 U.S.C. § 102(b) says copyright does not cover ideas, procedures, systems, methods or concepts, only how they are expressed.
